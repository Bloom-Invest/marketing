# Thread: Every Great Investment Was a Company Wall Street Hated
## Source: /Users/testuser/marketing/substack/drafts/12-mavericks/draft.md

### Post 1 (Hook)
Netflix at $7.50 in 2011. Every analyst was right — subscribers fleeing, margins compressing, cash burning.

They missed one thing: streaming subs growing 40%+ while DVD churned off.

$7.50 → $700. A 93x return. Right about everything except the only thing that mattered.

### Post 2
Every great investment of the last 20 years was a "maverick" — a company the consensus got fundamentally wrong.

Tesla at $45: "walking insolvency." 400,000 Model 3 deposits said otherwise. 20x.

Palantir at $7: "government consulting shop." Commercial revenue then grew 71% in a quarter. 17x.

### Post 3
The math of mavericks is wild.

5 bets, $1K each. 3 duds (lose 70%). 1 okay (+30%). 1 Netflix (10x).

Net return: +144%. Wrong on 3 out of 5 and you still crushed the S&P.

You don't need to be right often. You need to be right big.

### Post 4
The signal I look for: negative analyst sentiment + quietly accelerating fundamentals.

I call it the narrative gap.

Netflix 2011: CNBC said "streaming is unproven." The 10-K showed streaming subs up 40%, viewing hours tripling. That gap was worth 93x.

### Post 5
But here's the graveyard.

WeWork had every "maverick" trait. Unit economics never worked.

Peloton looked category-creating in 2020. $170 → $5.

Valeant: Bill Ackman put $4B behind it. $260 → $9.

When the data confirms the bear case instead of contradicting it — that's not a maverick. That's a bad stock with a good story.
