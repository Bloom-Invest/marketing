# Stop Watching EPS. Start Watching What Actually Moves the Stock.

*Most investors track the wrong numbers. Here's how to find the 2-3 metrics that explain 80% of a stock's price move.*

---

Chipotle reported Q3 2024 earnings on October 29th. They missed EPS by $0.27.

The stock went up 4%.

I pulled up the actual release. Same-store sales: up 6%. Transaction growth: positive for the fourth straight quarter. New restaurant openings: on track for 300+. Management *raised* full-year guidance.

Nobody gave a shit about the EPS miss. The metrics that actually drive Chipotle's stock — traffic, comps, and unit expansion — were all accelerating. Wall Street looked at the same report and said: "Who cares about the earnings number? The business is getting stronger."

That's when I got obsessed with what I call "key drivers."

## What Actually Moves Stock Prices

For most stocks, the price is driven by one, maybe two metrics. Not the 47 line items in the income statement. Not the "earnings beat" headline on CNBC. One or two specific numbers that the market has decided represent the health of *this particular business*.

For Chipotle, it's same-store sales and restaurant openings. That's it. That's the whole game.

For Amazon, it's AWS growth rate. AWS is ~17% of revenue but generates the vast majority of operating income. When AWS growth accelerates, the stock rips. When it decelerates — dropping from 37% to 12% in early 2023 — the stock gets crushed. The e-commerce side could be doing great. Doesn't matter.

For Netflix, it was paid subscriber net adds for a decade. Then in 2023 they pivoted to revenue growth and ARM (average revenue per member) as the ad tier launched. The driver *changed*. If you were still fixated on sub counts, you were watching the wrong scoreboard.

For CrowdStrike, it's net new ARR each quarter. A $90B company, and that single line item explains most post-earnings moves.

For Meta in 2023: operating expenses. Not ad revenue — everyone knew ads would recover. The question was whether Zuckerberg would stop lighting money on fire on the metaverse. He cut 21,000 employees. Operating margin went from 20% to 35% in three quarters. Stock tripled. The driver wasn't revenue. It was cost discipline.

**Every stock has a story, and the story is told through 1-2 specific metrics.** Everything else is noise.

## Why EPS Is the Wrong Metric

EPS is what CNBC puts on the screen. It's what your brokerage app highlights. It's what every "beat/miss" headline is about.

It's an *output*. Dozens of business decisions, accounting choices, tax rates, buybacks, and one-time items mashed into a single number. By the time something shows up in EPS, the market has already priced in the dynamics that caused it.

Buybacks alone inflate EPS 2-3% per year without the business improving at all. A company can "beat" earnings by repurchasing stock while revenue flatlines. That's not growth. That's financial engineering.

And the consensus game? Wall Street sets estimates, companies sandbag guidance, then everyone acts surprised by a $0.03 "beat." Theater.

What the smart money watches: the *inputs* that drive future earnings. The operational KPIs that tell you whether the business is gaining or losing momentum *right now*, before the accounting catches up.

## Finding Key Drivers the Hard Way

Before AI, identifying key drivers meant:

1. Pull 4-6 quarters of earnings call transcripts
2. Read each one (45-90 minutes of material per quarter)
3. Note what management leads with — they always lead with what they're proudest of
4. Note what analysts ask about first — that's what moves their models
5. Map the stock's after-hours reaction to which metrics surprised

Patterns emerge fast. Chipotle's CEO always opens with same-store sales. Amazon's CFO leads with AWS. CrowdStrike leads with ARR.

But 4-6 transcripts per stock is 4-5 hours of reading. Across a 15-stock portfolio? Sixty hours. That's why most investors default to EPS — it's easy, even though it's wrong.

## AI Agents Make This Trivially Fast

Here's what an AI agent does with those same transcripts in under 3 minutes:

**Keyword frequency across quarters.** Eight quarters of transcripts → ranked list of what management mentions most in prepared remarks. For Datadog, "ARR" and "large customers" (spending $100K+) dominate every call. Signal found.

**Emphasis detection.** Not just *what* management says — *where* they put it. When Chipotle's CEO moved "digital sales" from the opening paragraph to page three between Q2 and Q3 2023, that was a tell. The metric was decelerating. AI catches the *absence* of emphasis, not just its presence.

**Analyst question clustering.** If 6 of 10 analysts ask about the same metric in Q&A, that's what drives their models. Often different from what management wants to discuss — and that *gap* is signal.

**Reaction mapping.** Pair transcript data with the stock's 24-hour post-earnings move across multiple quarters. If subscriber adds consistently drive the biggest moves and revenue beats barely register, you've found your driver.

## When One Metric Explains Everything

**Meta, 2022-2024.** From $90 to $570. A 6x move. The single best predictor? Operating expenses. When Zuckerberg announced mass layoffs in November 2022 and followed through in April 2023, the market realized Meta could print cash if it stopped hemorrhaging money on the metaverse. OpEx dropped from $23B/quarter to $20B. Margins expanded from 20% to 35%+. One number. 6x return.

**Netflix, Q1 2022.** Lost 200,000 subscribers. Stock dropped 35% *in a single day*. Revenue was still growing. They were still profitable. None of it mattered. The market cared about one number, and it went negative.

Then Netflix launched the ad tier and cracked down on password sharing. Q3 2023: +8.76 million net adds — biggest quarter in years. Stock ripped. Same metric, opposite direction, massive move.

You didn't need a model. You needed to know which metric mattered and whether it was improving.

## Stop Watching Everything. Start Watching the Right Thing.

The biggest mistake isn't picking the wrong stocks. **It's watching the wrong metrics on the right stocks.**

You buy Amazon and obsess over total revenue when you should track AWS growth. You buy Netflix and panic about an EPS miss when net adds were fine. You buy Chipotle and don't realize same-store sales is the only number that matters.

One question: **"What does the market actually care about for this specific stock?"**

Watch that. Ignore the rest.
