# Your Portfolio Has a Blind Spot. AI Can Find It.

*You spent 10 hours building the bull case for your favorite stock. How long did you spend on the bear case?*

---

I was scrolling through my Bloom watchlist last Tuesday — 23 stocks, all neatly organized with research notes and thesis summaries. I felt prepared. Diligent. Like a real investor.

Then I counted my bear cases. Zero. Twenty-three stocks, every one with a detailed argument for why it would go up. Not one with a documented reason it might go down.

That's not a portfolio. That's a prayer dressed up as analysis.

## Confirmation Bias Isn't a Psychology Term — It's Your Default Setting

Everyone thinks they understand confirmation bias, and almost nobody thinks it applies to them.

"Yeah, confirmation bias is when people only look at evidence that supports their beliefs. But I'm rational. I do my research."

Bullshit. You do research that confirms the conclusion you already reached.

Think about how you actually find stocks. A friend mentions a ticker. You see it on fintwit. Someone on Reddit writes compelling DD. The stock's already up 40% and the chart looks gorgeous. You're *excited*. You want to buy it.

So you "research" it. You read the bull case threads. You find the analyst with the highest price target and nod along. You see insider buying and think *they know something*. You check the P/E, and if it's high, you rationalize: "well, for a company growing this fast…"

At no point did you actively seek out reasons the stock might be a terrible investment.

I did exactly this with Peloton in 2021. Revenue up 100%+! Recurring subscriptions! The future of fitness! I didn't seriously ask: what happens when gyms reopen? What if the TAM for $2,000 exercise bikes is a fraction of what everyone assumes? What if the unit economics fall apart at scale?

Those questions had obvious answers. I just didn't ask them. The stock went from $160 to $3.

Thirty thousand dollars in lessons. Expensive tuition for something an AI could've told me in thirty seconds.

## The Ecosystem Is Designed to Make You Bullish

It's not just psychology. The whole machine pushes you toward one side.

**Sell-side analysts are conflicted.** 89% of ratings are Buy or Hold. Fewer than 6% are Sell. Banks don't win investment banking mandates by telling the CEO their stock is overvalued.

**Financial media needs clicks.** "This stock could 10x!" gets engagement. "Significant downside risk due to margin compression" does not. CNBC doesn't run segments about the bear case for NVDA.

**Social media amplifies conviction.** FinTwit, Reddit, YouTube — communities organized around *excitement*. Nobody posts "here's why the stock I'm long might crash." You'd get ratio'd into oblivion.

**Your own portfolio creates bias.** Once you own a stock, you have a financial incentive to believe it's going up. Every piece of bad news gets rationalized. Every red day becomes a "buying opportunity." You're not analyzing anymore — you're rooting.

Ten bull arguments for every one bear argument. Then we wonder why retail portfolios underperform.

## Let AI Argue Against You

You can't effectively argue against your own thesis. You're emotionally compromised. It's like asking a defense attorney to also prosecute their client.

But an AI has no position in your stock. No feelings. No P&L to protect. It will happily rip your thesis apart — if you ask it to.

The trick is *how* you ask. "Analyze HIMS for me" gets a diplomatic, balanced summary. A little bull, a little bear, very safe. Useless.

Try this instead:

> *"I'm long HIMS at $55. I think it hits $80 in 12 months. Your job is to convince me I'm wrong. Be specific. Use numbers. Tell me what risks I'm not seeing. Pretend you're a short seller who's done their homework."*

That prompt produces something radically different. You're forcing an adversarial stance. A steel-manned bear case. The holes you're too invested to see.

The best AI-generated bear cases are the ones that make you angry. If you read the output and think "that's not fair" or "they're missing context" — that's your confirmation bias talking. The discomfort is the signal.

## The Framework: Bull vs. Bear Done Right

A good bull/bear analysis isn't "it could go up or down." That's a weather forecast. You need structure.

**Specific, falsifiable claims on each side.** Not hand-waving — concrete catalysts with timelines.

- Bull: "HIMS revenue will grow 40%+ in FY2025 driven by GLP-1 compounding demand."
- Bear: "FDA will crack down on compounding pharmacies by Q3 2025, eliminating HIMS's highest-growth product line."

Both testable. Both reference real events.

**Three price targets.** Bull (everything goes right), base (inline), bear (key risks materialize). Then do the math: if your bull case gives 50% upside and your bear case gives 50% downside, your risk/reward is 1:1. That's a coin flip, not an investment thesis.

**Pre-commitments — the part nobody does.** Before you buy, write down what would make you sell. Not "if it drops 20%" — that's a stop-loss, not a thesis change. Something like:

- "If HIMS subscriber growth drops below 15% any quarter, I'm out."
- "If the FDA bans their compounded GLP-1 products, I halve my position."
- "If insider selling exceeds $50M in six months, I re-evaluate."

These are contracts with your future self. They exist to protect you from the version of you that rationalizes every piece of bad news once you're emotionally attached.

## Real Example: HIMS, Start to Finish

I went through this exercise with Hims & Hers on [Bloom](https://investwithbloom.com). Here's what both sides look like when you actually do the work.

### Bull Case → Target: $85

Revenue: $1.5B trailing twelve months, growing 69% YoY. That's not "solid growth" — that's face-melting. Gross margins at 82% — software-level economics on a company selling health products. The subscription model (2M+ paying monthly) means revenue is recurring and predictable. Churn is low. LTV/CAC is improving.

The GLP-1 play is enormous. Compounded semaglutide at a fraction of brand-name Wegovy/Ozempic pricing into a $100B+ weight loss market. Valuation: ~30x forward earnings for 69% growth. Not cheap, but for this growth rate, arguably reasonable. Multiple insiders bought shares last quarter.

### Bear Case → Target: $28

The GLP-1 story is a regulatory time bomb. The FDA has been signaling a crackdown on compounding pharmacies, and Novo Nordisk is lobbying aggressively to shut them down. If rules tighten, HIMS loses its fastest-growing product line overnight. This isn't hypothetical — it's an active regulatory proceeding.

Revenue concentration is the deeper problem. Strip out GLP-1 and growth drops to maybe 25-30%. Still decent. Not the same stock. Not the same multiple.

Competition is closing in. Amazon Pharmacy, Teladoc, Ro — all expanding into the same space. HIMS has brand recognition but no real moat. Nothing proprietary about telehealth consultations and shipping generic drugs. Customer acquisition costs are rising as they scale into a more crowded market.

And the stock already tripled. When something is this consensus-bullish, ask yourself: who's the next buyer?

### The Verdict

50% upside vs. 50% downside. Symmetrical risk/reward — which means the bull case better be significantly more probable, or this isn't a good bet.

My honest assessment: the regulatory risk is real but probably not a total wipeout. Even if compounded semaglutide gets restricted, HIMS has a growing base business. But the stock price already embeds a lot of optimism.

I added HIMS to my watchlist but didn't buy. The bear case didn't kill the thesis — it made me realize I needed a better entry point. Without this exercise, I would've bought on excitement alone.

## The Bear Case Is Where the Alpha Lives

Not because bear cases are more often correct — they're not. Stocks go up most of the time.

But the bear case changes your *behavior* in three ways that compound:

**It improves your entry points.** "I'd buy HIMS at $40 because the base business alone justifies that valuation." That's investing. "I'll buy it because it's going up" is gambling.

**It prepares you for drawdowns.** When your stock drops 30%, you'll panic — unless you already mapped the scenarios that could cause it. If the drop matches a risk you documented, you evaluate calmly: has my thesis changed, or is this noise?

**It helps you size positions.** Strong bull + weak bear = bigger allocation. Strong bull + equally strong bear = smaller bet. You can't make this call looking at one side.

The investors who compound wealth over decades spend more time on the bear case than the bull case. The bull case gets you interested. The bear case keeps you from blowing up.

## The Uncomfortable Part

Using AI to argue against your own positions sucks. Nobody wants to hear that the stock they love might be a bad investment.

That discomfort is the entire point.

If your thesis can't survive a well-argued bear case, it was never a good thesis. You were just confident. And confidence without stress-testing is just vibes with a brokerage account.

Build the bull case. Then let AI destroy it. Whatever survives? That's your actual thesis.
